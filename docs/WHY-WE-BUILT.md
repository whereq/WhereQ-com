# Why I Built WhereQ.com

### Every Place Has a Story. I Wanted to Make Those Stories Discoverable.

The idea behind WhereQ.com didn't come from a business plan. It started with
curiosity, a map, and a few observations about how people interact with the
places around them.

**It started in 2012, when I first came across Waze.**

Waze introduced me to a different way of thinking about maps. A map didn't
have to be just a collection of roads, streets, and coordinates. It could
become a living source of information, enriched by what was happening around
those locations.

That got me thinking:

> What if a location were more than just a latitude and longitude?

A place could carry much more information and much more value. It could tell
us what's happening nearby, what services are available, what's changing, and
what might matter to the people who live, work, or visit there.

For example, knowing the coordinates of a shopping mall is useful. But knowing
what's happening inside it, what's on sale, which restaurants are popular,
what's happening in the surrounding neighborhood, and how far it is from you
makes that location far more meaningful.

**A location is not just a point on a map. It's a context.**

### 🏘️ Thinking About Neighborhoods, Not Just Addresses

After moving to Canada, I became increasingly interested in how Canadian
towns and cities are planned and organized.

In particular, I found the concept of suburban communities fascinating.

A typical suburb is not simply a collection of houses. It often has a natural
structure: residential streets, a neighborhood, a shopping center, schools,
parks, community facilities, and the places people visit in their everyday
lives.

People living in the same neighborhood often care about many of the same
things.

What's happening around the community? Is there a local event this weekend?
Has a nearby store announced a promotion? Is there road construction? Are
there new services opening nearby? What's happening at the local shopping
center?

These questions are often more relevant to residents than what's happening
somewhere else in the city.

That led me to another idea: **geographic boundaries matter.**

If we can define a meaningful geographic area — a neighborhood, a community,
or a suburb — we can organize information around the places people actually
identify with.

Instead of making people search through an entire city, why not bring the
information that matters directly to their neighborhood?

A well-defined boundary can turn scattered location-based information into
something much more useful and personal.

### 📍 The Distance Question

There is another small habit that helped shape this idea.

Sometimes, I come across a piece of news, a local announcement, or an
attractive discount. Before getting too excited, my first instinct is to
check where it is.

> How far is that place from me?

Is it five minutes away, fifteen minutes away, or an hour away?

Is it somewhere I can conveniently visit, or is it simply too far to be
practical?

I suspect I'm not the only person who thinks this way.

A promotion might look attractive, but its actual value depends partly on
where it is and how accessible it is.

A local event might be interesting, but its relevance changes significantly
depending on the distance.

Information without location is often incomplete. And location without
distance or geographic context is often not enough.

### 🚀 Bringing These Ideas Together: WhereQ.com

These observations eventually came together in one simple idea:

> **What if we could connect information, places, geographic areas, and
> distance in one experience?**

That's why I built WhereQ.com.

WhereQ.com is a location-centric information platform designed to make
information more meaningful through maps, locations, geographic boundaries,
and proximity.

Rather than treating a map as merely a navigation tool, I see it as a way to
discover, organize, and understand the world around us.

It's about helping people answer questions such as:

- What's happening around me?
- What's interesting in my neighborhood?
- What services, stores, and activities are nearby?
- How far away is that place?
- What's happening within a particular community or geographic area?

The idea is simple, but its possibilities are broad.

Because almost every piece of real-world information has a geographic
dimension. News, events, businesses, services, promotions, communities, and
everyday activities all happen somewhere.

And knowing *where* something happens can change how useful that information
becomes.

### 💡 The Bigger Picture

I didn't build WhereQ.com because I thought the world needed another map
application.

I built it because I believe location is an underused dimension of
information.

We already have plenty of information. We have search engines, social
networks, news platforms, business directories, and countless applications.

But information is often disconnected from the places where it actually
matters.

I want to explore a different approach: make location a first-class part of
how information is discovered and experienced.

**Because sometimes, the most important question isn't just "What is
happening?"**

**It's "Where is it happening, and how close is it to me?"**

That's the thinking behind WhereQ.com.

And it's a journey I'm still building, one location at a time.
